SafeStep: An Agentic AI Assistant for Digital Safety

Internship project developed using NeuralSeek's mAIstro platform

During my internship at NeuralSeek, I conceived, designed, and built SafeStep, an AI assistant intended to help older adults and non-technical users navigate everyday digital tasks more safely and independently.

I developed the project from the initial idea through the final presentation. My work included user interviews, defining the use case, designing the agent workflow, writing and revising prompts, working with Python-based classification logic, testing conversations, evaluating responses, collaborating with other interns, and presenting SafeStep to NeuralSeek.

Project Overview

Many routine activities now depend on digital tools. People use phones and websites to communicate, manage appointments, access healthcare information, pay bills, shop, send money, and manage accounts. These systems are convenient for experienced users, but they often assume that a person already understands technical vocabulary, interface conventions, account security, and online safety.

I became interested in how an AI assistant could make some of these tasks less intimidating. My goal was not to create another general-purpose chatbot. SafeStep was intended for people who might need more explanation, shorter steps, or extra guidance when dealing with unfamiliar technology.

Safety was part of the idea from the beginning. A person asking how to change the volume on a phone does not need the same response strategy as someone asking whether to click a link in a message claiming that a bank account has been locked. I wanted SafeStep to recognize that difference and respond more cautiously when money, passwords, suspicious links, or personal information were involved.

Design question: How can an AI assistant make digital tasks easier to understand while still encouraging users to stop and verify information when a situation may be unsafe?

User Research and Interviews

Before finalizing the design, I conducted informal interviews with people of different ages and backgrounds who I knew had experienced some difficulty with technology. I wanted to understand what kinds of problems they actually encountered instead of deciding on SafeStep's features only from my own assumptions.

I asked participants how they understood AI-powered digital assistants, which tasks they found difficult on their phones or computers, and what they would want an assistant to help them with.

The answers covered a wide range of problems. Participants mentioned online payments and money transfers, sending and forwarding emails, changing passwords, dealing with unwanted pages on a phone, completing login or authentication tasks, and deciding whether a website or someone contacting them online could be trusted.

One response that stood out to me was frustration with AI assistance that felt too general. The participant explained that broad or generic responses were especially frustrating when the person asking the question already had limited experience using electronic devices.

That feedback influenced how I approached SafeStep's response style. I wanted the agent to focus on the immediate problem, give specific steps, and avoid assuming that the user already knew where settings or features were located.

The interviews also showed that not everyone was interested in using an AI assistant. Some participants preferred interacting with a person. I thought that was important feedback rather than something the project needed to overcome. It reinforced my decision to treat SafeStep as another source of support, not as a replacement for human help.

What I took from the interviews: SafeStep needed to be specific, patient, easy to follow, and especially careful around payments, passwords, unfamiliar websites, and situations where the user was unsure whether something could be trusted.

Project Goals

I designed SafeStep around several goals:

  • Explain common phone and digital tasks using plain language.
  • Break complicated procedures into short, ordered steps.
  • Avoid unnecessary technical terminology.
  • Help users recognize common signs of scams and phishing.
  • Apply additional caution to questions involving money, passwords, downloads, personal information, or suspicious links.
  • Avoid claiming that a website, message, or transaction is completely safe.
  • Maintain a patient and nonjudgmental tone for users who may not feel comfortable with technology.

Agent Architecture and Workflow

SafeStep was not implemented as one large prompt. I used a modular workflow in mAIstro so that different stages could perform different jobs before the final response reached the user.

Figure 1. SafeStep's mAIstro workflow. The workflow includes user input, privacy and PII processing, session history, contextual instructions, Python-based classification, knowledge retrieval, workflow variables, safety guidance, and LLM response generation.

Main Workflow Components

  1. User input: The workflow receives the user's question about a phone, application, website, message, payment, or another digital task.
  2. Protection and PII processing: Protect, Identify PII, and Remove PII components help reduce unnecessary exposure of sensitive personal information.
  3. Session history: Previous conversation information can be carried forward so that the agent has context for follow-up questions.
  4. Agent context: An Add Context component defines SafeStep's role, audience, and communication style.
  5. Task and risk classification: A Python Sandbox analyzes the text and assigns a task category and risk level.
  6. Knowledge retrieval: Virtual Knowledge Base components make relevant reference information available to later stages of the workflow.
  7. Workflow variables: Variables such as the user message, task category, risk level, knowledge result, and safety flag are passed between different parts of the agent.
  8. Safety guidance: Additional instructions are applied to requests involving suspicious links, payments, passwords, scams, or other potentially risky actions.
  9. LLM response: The final model receives the processed user message together with information created by earlier stages and generates the response.

Python-Based Task and Risk Classification

One part of the workflow that gave me an opportunity to use code was the Python Sandbox. I used rule-based logic to identify the general type of request and determine whether stronger safety guidance should be applied.

The classifier begins with a low-risk general-help category. Certain words or combinations of words change the task category and risk level. 

task_category = "general_cellphone_help"
risk_level = "low"
needs_safety_guidance = False

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "app", "download", "install", "open", "update"
]):
    task_category = "using_apps"

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "text", "message", "sms", "photo", "picture"
]):
    task_category = "sending_messages"

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "pay", "payment", "bank", "transfer", "card",
    "venmo", "zelle", "cash app", "apple pay"
]):
    task_category = "mobile_payments"
    risk_level = "medium"

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "link", "website", "url", "click", "browser", "site"
]):
    task_category = "suspicious_links"
    risk_level = "high"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "scam", "phishing", "verify account", "urgent",
    "locked", "suspended", "password", "security alert",
    "fraud", "confirm your account", "bank account"
]):
    task_category = "scam_phishing"
    risk_level = "high"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

if (
    any(word in message_lower for word in [
        "pay", "payment", "bank", "card", "transfer"
    ])
    and
    any(word in message_lower for word in [
        "link", "website", "click", "url"
    ])
):
    task_category = "scam_phishing"
    risk_level = "high"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

I liked using deterministic rules for this stage because the decisions were easy to inspect. If a message was labeled high risk, I could see which terms contributed to that result instead of treating the classification as a black box.

The approach also has limitations. Keyword rules cannot capture every way someone might describe a scam, payment problem, or suspicious interaction. A future version could compare this logic with a classifier trained on a larger labeled set of digital-help questions.

Prompt Engineering and Workflow Variables

Prompt engineering was important because SafeStep needed a specific communication style. The assistant should not simply return everything it knows. It should focus on the immediate problem and present the information in a way that is easy to follow.

The final LLM step receives several pieces of information created earlier in the workflow. A simplified version of the prompt looks like this: 

User message:
<< name: user_message, prompt: false >>

Task category:
<< name: task_category, prompt: false >>

Risk level:
<< name: risk_level, prompt: false >>

Needs safety guidance:
<< name: needs_safety_guidance, prompt: false >>

Safety guidance:
<< name: safety_guidance, prompt: false >>

Knowledge base help:
<< name: kb_result, prompt: false >>

Please answer the user in plain language with short numbered steps.
If the situation is risky, include a short warning and do not
guarantee safety.

Using separate variables made the workflow easier to inspect because I could check the user message, risk level, knowledge result, and safety instructions independently before they reached the LLM.

Testing and Iterative Refinement

I tested SafeStep with different types of questions instead of relying on one successful example. I used ordinary phone-help questions, payment questions, and higher-risk situations involving suspicious messages and passwords.

Some of the most useful tests were the ones that did not work exactly as I expected. Those examples helped me find weaknesses in the workflow and decide what needed to be changed.

Phishing Scenario

One test involved a message claiming that a bank account had been locked and asking the user to click a link and enter a password. SafeStep correctly told the user not to click the link or provide the password and recommended contacting the bank through an official channel.

Figure 2. SafeStep responding to a simulated bank phishing scenario.

The test also exposed a problem. The response included example contact information that had not been verified. I did not want the model generating email addresses, phone numbers, or URLs that might not be correct.

I revised the safety instructions so that SafeStep would instead direct the user to verified information from an official application, website, card, statement, or another trusted source.

Testing Incomplete Input

I also tested what happened when the workflow did not receive a complete question. In this case, the agent asked the user to explain what they needed help with rather than guessing.

Figure 3. SafeStep asking for more information when the input did not contain a specific task.

This example became useful while I was debugging input handling because it helped me determine whether the user's message was actually reaching the final LLM stage.

Payment Scenario

Another test asked SafeStep how to transfer money through Zelle. This was a useful scenario because a payment question is not automatically a scam, but it still deserves more caution than a basic question about phone settings.

Figure 4. A payment-related test used to evaluate SafeStep's instructions and financial-safety guidance.

This response also showed why generated answers have to be checked for more than readability. The answer was clear and organized, but some product-specific information was outdated.

That result made me pay more attention to knowledge grounding and to the difference between information coming from the model itself and information supported by current reference material.

Debugging the Workflow

One of the more technical problems I encountered involved variables moving between mAIstro components. During testing, values such as task_category, risk_level, and kb_result were initially reaching the final LLM as unresolved placeholders instead of the values produced earlier in the workflow.

I used the mAIstro evaluation view to inspect the workflow one stage at a time. This allowed me to see what was actually being passed to the model rather than assuming that the configuration was working correctly.

I corrected the variable references so that stored values were retrieved with syntax such as: 

<< name: risk_level, prompt: false >>

I also had to distinguish between collecting a new runtime input and using an existing value later in the workflow. The initial user input can be requested as a parameter, while later stages should retrieve the existing user_message.

While troubleshooting, I kept LLM caching disabled so that each evaluation reflected my newest changes. This gave me a clearer understanding of how state is passed between mAIstro nodes and how a small configuration problem can affect the final model output.

Safety and Responsible AI

SafeStep was designed as a support tool rather than a replacement for human judgment or professional cybersecurity assistance. An AI model can recognize patterns associated with common scams, but it cannot guarantee that every website, message, or transaction is safe or unsafe.

Suspicious Links

SafeStep encourages users not to open unexpected links and to verify requests through a known official channel.

Passwords and Verification Codes

The agent warns against sharing passwords and one-time verification codes during unexpected interactions.

Payments

Questions involving money receive additional caution, especially when urgency, unfamiliar recipients, or suspicious links are involved.

Uncertainty

SafeStep is instructed to explain warning signs and verification steps rather than guarantee that something is safe.

Challenges and What I Learned

Making Instructions Simple Without Removing Important Information

One challenge was deciding how much information to provide. Very long answers could defeat the purpose of an assistant intended for users who already feel overwhelmed by technology. At the same time, simplifying a procedure too much could leave out a necessary step.

I addressed this by emphasizing short instructions and prioritizing the immediate task rather than explaining every possible option at once.

Managing Safety Without Treating Every Question as a Scam

A person asking what Zelle is needs useful information. Someone being told to enter a banking password through an unexpected link needs a much stronger warning. The task and risk variables gave me a way to represent that difference.

Evaluating More Than Fluency

Testing showed me that a well-written answer can still contain a factual or safety problem. This became especially clear when the model generated unverified contact details and when it returned outdated information about a payment service.

I learned to evaluate responses for clarity, relevance, factual grounding, and safety rather than judging them only by how natural they sounded.

Debugging a Multi-Stage AI System

SafeStep also changed the way I think about LLM applications. The model is only one part of the system. Input handling, privacy processing, classification, retrieval, variables, and prompt construction all affect what the final model sees.

Debugging the workflow taught me to inspect intermediate steps rather than immediately rewriting the final prompt whenever something went wrong.

Reflection and Future Work

SafeStep gave me the opportunity to take ownership of an AI project from the initial idea through user interviews, design, implementation, testing, refinement, and presentation.

One of the biggest lessons for me was that building a useful AI assistant involves much more than choosing a language model. The quality of the final response depends on everything that happens before the LLM is called: how the input is handled, what information is removed or preserved, how the request is classified, what knowledge is retrieved, and what instructions are included in the prompt.

I also changed the way I think about prompt engineering. I now see prompts as one part of a larger system. They work together with code, variables, retrieval, safety rules, and evaluation. A good prompt cannot compensate for incorrect information moving through the rest of the workflow.

If I continued developing SafeStep, I would create a larger evaluation set containing general phone questions, payment questions, phishing attempts, suspicious links, and ambiguous requests. I would use that set to measure the performance of the risk classifier and identify false positives and false negatives.

I would also conduct more structured usability testing with people who have different levels of technical experience. The initial interviews helped me understand the problem, but testing the working agent with users would make it possible to measure whether the instructions actually help people complete tasks successfully.

Other future work would include multilingual testing, stronger knowledge-base grounding, and systematic evaluation of whether retrieved sources support the answers generated by the model.

SafeStep strengthened my ability to connect human language technology with a specific user problem. It also taught me to look critically at AI output rather than assuming that a response is good simply because it sounds confident or natural.

Internship project developed using NeuralSeek's mAIstro platform

During my internship at NeuralSeek, I conceived, designed, and built SafeStep, an AI assistant intended to help older adults and non-technical users navigate everyday digital tasks more safely and independently.

I developed the project from the initial idea through the final presentation. My work included interviewing potential users, defining the use case, designing the agent workflow, writing and revising prompts, working with Python-based task and risk classification, testing different inputs, evaluating responses, collaborating with other interns, and presenting SafeStep to NeuralSeek.

Project Overview

Many routine activities now depend on digital tools. People use phones and websites to communicate, manage appointments, access healthcare information, pay bills, shop, send money, and manage accounts. These systems are convenient for experienced users, but they often assume that a person already understands technical vocabulary, interface conventions, account security, and online safety.

I became interested in how an AI assistant could make some of these tasks less intimidating. My goal was not to create another general-purpose chatbot. SafeStep was intended for people who might need more explanation, shorter steps, or extra guidance when dealing with unfamiliar technology.

Safety was part of the idea from the beginning. A person asking how to change a phone setting does not need the same kind of response as someone asking whether to click a link in a message claiming that a bank account has been locked. I wanted SafeStep to recognize that difference and respond more cautiously when money, passwords, suspicious links, or personal information were involved.

Design question: How can an AI assistant make digital tasks easier to understand while still encouraging users to stop and verify information when a situation may be unsafe?

What is mAIstro?

I built SafeStep in mAIstro, NeuralSeek's visual environment for building AI agents. Instead of writing one program that handles everything from beginning to end, mAIstro allows an agent to be organized as a workflow made up of connected components. Each component has a particular purpose, and the output of one step can be passed to another step later in the workflow.

This structure worked well for SafeStep because I wanted more control over what happened before a question reached the final language model. The workflow could process personal information, preserve conversation history, classify the request, retrieve relevant knowledge, add safety instructions, and then send the resulting information to the LLM.

User Research and Interviews

Before finalizing the design, I conducted informal interviews with people of different ages and backgrounds who I knew had experienced some difficulty with technology. I wanted to understand what kinds of problems they actually encountered instead of deciding on SafeStep's features only from my own assumptions.

I asked participants how they understood AI-powered digital assistants, which tasks they found difficult on their phones or computers, and what they would want an assistant to help them with.

The answers covered different types of problems. Participants mentioned online payments and money transfers, sending and forwarding emails, changing passwords, dealing with unwanted pages on a phone, completing login or authentication tasks, and deciding whether a website or someone contacting them online could be trusted.

One response that stood out to me was frustration with AI assistance that felt too general. The participant explained that broad responses were especially frustrating when the person asking the question already had limited experience using electronic devices.

That feedback influenced how I approached SafeStep's response style. I wanted the agent to focus on the immediate problem, give specific steps, and avoid assuming that the user already knew where a setting or feature was located.

The interviews also showed that not everyone was interested in using an AI assistant. Some participants preferred talking to a person. I thought that was important feedback. It reinforced my decision to treat SafeStep as another source of support rather than a replacement for human help.

Overall, the interviews helped me see that SafeStep needed to be specific, patient, easy to follow, and especially careful around payments, passwords, unfamiliar websites, and situations where the user was unsure whether something could be trusted.

Project Goals

I designed SafeStep around several goals:

  • Explain common phone and digital tasks using plain language.
  • Break complicated procedures into short, ordered steps.
  • Avoid unnecessary technical terminology.
  • Help users recognize common signs of scams and phishing.
  • Apply additional caution to questions involving money, passwords, downloads, personal information, or suspicious links.
  • Avoid claiming that a website, message, or transaction is completely safe.
  • Maintain a patient and nonjudgmental tone for users who may not feel comfortable with technology.

Agent Architecture and Workflow

SafeStep uses a sequence of connected components rather than sending the user's question directly to an LLM. I used this structure so that information could be processed and checked at several points before the final answer was generated.

Figure 1. SafeStep's mAIstro workflow, showing the components used to process user input before the final response is generated.

Main Workflow Components

  1. User input: The workflow receives a question about a phone, application, website, message, payment, or another digital task.
  2. Protection and PII processing: Protect, Identify PII, and Remove PII components help reduce unnecessary exposure of sensitive personal information.
  3. Session history: Conversation history can be carried forward so that the agent has context for follow-up questions.
  4. Agent context: An Add Context component establishes the agent's purpose, audience, and expected communication style.
  5. Python classification: A Python Sandbox examines the user's message and assigns information such as task category and risk level.
  6. Knowledge retrieval: Virtual Knowledge Base components make relevant reference information available to later stages.
  7. Workflow variables: Values such as the user message, task category, risk level, knowledge result, and safety flag can be stored and passed to later components.
  8. Safety guidance: Additional instructions can be added for questions involving suspicious links, payments, passwords, scams, or other higher-risk situations.
  9. LLM response: The final model receives the information created by the earlier steps and generates the response shown to the user.

The completed agent can also be exported from mAIstro as a .dat file. The exported file preserves the agent's workflow and configuration so that it can later be imported into mAIstro. It is not a standalone application and is intended to be opened through the mAIstro environment.

Python Sandbox

The Python Sandbox is one of the parts of SafeStep where I used explicit program logic. Its job is to examine the user's message before the final LLM response is generated.

Figure 2. Python Sandbox code used to classify SafeStep requests and assign a risk level before the final response is generated.

The code first receives the user message and converts it to lowercase so that the same rules can be applied regardless of capitalization. It starts with a general cellphone-help category and a low risk level. It then checks the message for groups of terms associated with applications, text messages, payments, links, scams, phishing, passwords, and other situations.

For example, a question containing words such as payment, bank, or Zelle can be categorized as a mobile payment question. A message involving a suspicious link or phishing language receives a higher risk level and sets the safety-guidance flag to True.

The Sandbox then outputs the resulting values as JSON so that the information can be used by later parts of the workflow.

Task and Risk Classification Code

try:
    msg = user_message
except NameError:
    try:
        msg = message
    except NameError:
        try:
            msg = text
        except NameError:
            try:
                msg = input
            except NameError:
                msg = ""

user_message = str(msg)
message_lower = user_message.lower()

task_category = "general_cellphone_help"
risk_level = "low"
needs_safety_guidance = False

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "app", "download", "install", "open", "update"
]):
    task_category = "using_apps"

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "text", "message", "sms", "photo", "picture"
]):
    task_category = "sending_messages"

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "pay", "payment", "bank", "transfer", "card",
    "venmo", "zelle", "cash app", "apple pay"
]):
    task_category = "mobile_payments"
    risk_level = "medium"

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "link", "website", "url", "click", "browser", "site"
]):
    task_category = "suspicious_links"
    risk_level = "high"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

if any(word in message_lower for word in [
    "scam", "phishing", "verify account", "urgent",
    "locked", "suspended", "password", "security alert",
    "fraud", "confirm your account", "bank account"
]):
    task_category = "scam_phishing"
    risk_level = "high"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

if (
    any(word in message_lower for word in [
        "pay", "payment", "bank", "card", "transfer"
    ])
    and
    any(word in message_lower for word in [
        "link", "website", "click", "url"
    ])
):
    task_category = "scam_phishing"
    risk_level = "high"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

import json

print(json.dumps({
    "user_message": user_message,
    "task_category": task_category,
    "risk_level": risk_level,
    "needs_safety_guidance": needs_safety_guidance
}))

I chose simple deterministic rules for this stage because I could see exactly why a message received a particular category or risk level. It also made the logic easier to test.

The limitation is that keyword rules cannot capture every way a person might describe a scam or technical problem. A future version could compare this approach with a classifier trained on a larger set of labeled examples.

Prompt Engineering and Workflow Variables

Prompt engineering was another important part of the project. I wanted the response to be understandable to someone who might not know technical terms, but I also wanted the model to use information created earlier in the workflow.

Instead of sending only the question to the final model, the prompt can include the user message, task category, risk level, safety guidance, and knowledge-base result. 

User message:
<< name: user_message, prompt: false >>

Task category:
<< name: task_category, prompt: false >>

Risk level:
<< name: risk_level, prompt: false >>

Needs safety guidance:
<< name: needs_safety_guidance, prompt: false >>

Safety guidance:
<< name: safety_guidance, prompt: false >>

Knowledge base help:
<< name: kb_result, prompt: false >>

Please answer the user in plain language with short numbered steps.
If the situation is risky, include a short warning and do not
guarantee safety.

Keeping these values separate made the workflow easier for me to inspect and debug. I could check whether a problem came from the original input, classification, safety logic, retrieved knowledge, or final response.

Testing with Different Inputs

I tested SafeStep using different kinds of input because one successful answer was not enough to tell me whether the workflow was behaving consistently. I used three types of scenarios: an ordinary or incomplete request, a question involving a money transfer through Zelle, and a higher-risk message asking the user to click a link and provide a banking password.

The inputs were chosen to exercise different parts of the workflow. An incomplete request tested whether the agent would ask for more information instead of guessing. The Zelle question tested how it handled a legitimate financial task that still called for some caution. The phishing-style message tested whether stronger safety guidance would appear when the input contained more serious warning signs.

I intentionally used different kinds of questions because I wanted to see how the agent behaved when the user needed clarification, when a question involved a normal financial task, and when the input contained warning signs associated with phishing.

The tests also showed me that a response could sound clear while still having a problem. One response included contact information that had not been verified, while another included product-specific information that was no longer current. These examples reminded me that fluency alone is not a good measure of response quality.

I adjusted the safety instructions so that the agent would not invent phone numbers, email addresses, URLs, or other organizational contact details. Instead, users should be directed to contact information obtained through an official application, website, card, statement, or another verified source.

Debugging the Workflow

One technical problem I encountered involved variables moving between mAIstro components. During testing, values such as task_category, risk_level, and kb_result were sometimes reaching the final LLM as unresolved placeholders instead of the values produced by earlier steps.

I used the mAIstro evaluation view to inspect the workflow stage by stage. That made it possible to see the actual prompt being sent to the model and compare it with what I expected the workflow to produce.

I corrected the variable references so that stored values could be retrieved with syntax such as: 

<< name: risk_level, prompt: false >>

I also had to separate the point where the user enters a question from the places where that same value is reused later. This helped me understand the difference between requesting a runtime parameter and retrieving an existing workflow variable.

While testing changes, I kept LLM caching disabled so that each run reflected the most recent configuration. Working through these issues gave me a better understanding of how state moves between components in an agentic workflow.

Safety and Responsible AI

SafeStep was designed as a support tool rather than a replacement for human judgment or professional cybersecurity assistance. An AI model can recognize patterns associated with common scams, but it cannot guarantee that every website, message, or transaction is safe or unsafe.

Suspicious Links

SafeStep encourages users not to open unexpected links and to verify requests through a known official channel.

Passwords and Verification Codes

The agent warns against sharing passwords and one-time verification codes during unexpected interactions.

Payments

Questions involving money receive additional caution, especially when urgency, unfamiliar recipients, or suspicious links are involved.

Uncertainty

The agent is instructed to explain warning signs and verification steps rather than guarantee that something is safe.

Challenges and What I Learned

Making Instructions Simple Without Removing Important Information

One challenge was deciding how much information to provide. Very long answers could defeat the purpose of an assistant intended for users who already feel overwhelmed by technology. At the same time, simplifying a procedure too much could leave out something important.

I addressed this by emphasizing short instructions and prioritizing the immediate task rather than explaining every possible option at once.

Managing Different Levels of Risk

Not every question involving money is a scam. A person asking how Zelle works needs useful instructions, while someone being told to enter a banking password through an unexpected link needs a much stronger warning.

The task category and risk variables gave me a way to represent that difference in the workflow.

Evaluating More Than Fluency

Testing showed me that a well-written answer can still contain a factual or safety problem. I learned to look at clarity, relevance, factual grounding, and safety instead of judging an answer only by how natural it sounded.

Debugging a Multi-Stage AI System

SafeStep also changed how I think about LLM applications. The language model is only one part of the system. Input handling, privacy processing, classification, retrieval, variables, and prompt construction all affect what the final model receives.

Debugging the workflow taught me to inspect intermediate steps rather than immediately changing the final prompt whenever something went wrong.

Reflection and Future Work

SafeStep gave me the opportunity to take ownership of an AI project from the initial idea through user interviews, design, implementation, testing, debugging, and presentation.

One of the biggest lessons for me was that building a useful AI assistant involves much more than choosing a language model. The final response depends on everything that happens before the LLM is called: how the input is handled, what information is removed or preserved, how the request is classified, what knowledge is retrieved, and what instructions are passed to the model.

I also changed how I think about prompt engineering. I now see a prompt as one part of a larger system. It works together with code, variables, retrieval, safety rules, and evaluation. A good prompt cannot compensate for incorrect information moving through the rest of the workflow.

If I continued developing SafeStep, I would create a larger evaluation set with general phone questions, payment questions, phishing attempts, suspicious links, and ambiguous requests. I would use that set to measure the task and risk classifier more systematically and identify false positives and false negatives.

I would also conduct more structured usability testing with people who have different levels of technical experience. The initial interviews helped me understand the problem, but testing the working agent with users would show whether the instructions actually help people complete tasks.

Other future work would include multilingual testing, stronger knowledge-base grounding, and more systematic evaluation of whether retrieved sources support the answers generated by the model.

SafeStep strengthened my ability to connect human language technology with a specific user problem. It also taught me to look critically at AI output instead of assuming that a response is good simply because it sounds confident or natural.

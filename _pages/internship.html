SafeStep: An Agentic AI Assistant for Digital Safety

Internship project developed using NeuralSeek's mAIstro platform

During my internship at NeuralSeek, I conceived, designed, and built SafeStep, an AI-powered digital assistant intended to help older adults and non-technical users navigate smartphones, computers, websites, and online services more safely and independently.

I developed the project from its initial concept through its final company presentation. My work included defining the use case, designing the agent workflow, writing and refining prompts, testing conversations, evaluating responses, collaborating with other interns, and presenting the completed solution to NeuralSeek.

Project Overview

Digital services are increasingly necessary for communication, healthcare, banking, shopping, account management, and access to public resources. However, many digital systems assume that users already understand technical terminology, interface conventions, account security, and online safety. These assumptions can create barriers for older adults and people with limited experience using smartphones or computers.

I created SafeStep to address this problem through a conversational interface. The agent was designed to provide patient, step-by-step guidance for common digital tasks while also helping users identify suspicious messages, potentially unsafe websites, phishing attempts, and other signs of online fraud.

The project combined agentic AI design with a human-centered objective: helping users complete digital tasks without making them feel embarrassed, rushed, or overwhelmed. SafeStep was therefore designed not only to provide information, but also to adapt its explanations to the user's level of technical familiarity.

Problem Statement

People with limited digital experience may struggle to interpret unfamiliar icons, menus, security warnings, payment screens, account settings, or technical instructions. Even a routine task can become difficult when an interface changes or when instructions use specialized vocabulary.

Online scams create an additional risk. A user who is uncertain about how a legitimate website, message, or payment process should behave may have difficulty distinguishing normal interactions from phishing or fraudulent requests. Fear of making a mistake can also discourage users from attempting digital tasks independently.

Design question: How can an AI agent provide accessible digital guidance while encouraging users to remain cautious and avoid overreliance on automated advice?

Project Goals

The main goals of SafeStep were to:

  • Explain smartphone, computer, application, and website tasks in plain language.
  • Break complicated procedures into manageable, sequential steps.
  • Help users recognize warning signs associated with scams and phishing.
  • Support safer decision-making during online payments and account interactions.
  • Adapt explanations to different levels of technical experience.
  • Increase users' confidence and independence when accessing digital services.
  • Maintain a calm, patient, supportive, and nonjudgmental conversational style.

Agent Architecture and Workflow

SafeStep was implemented as a modular workflow in the mAIstro platform. Each component performs a specific function before the final response is returned to the user.

Figure 1. SafeStep workflow developed in NeuralSeek's mAIstro platform. The workflow includes privacy protection, personally identifiable information processing, session history, contextual instructions, Python-based task classification, knowledge-base retrieval, variables, safety guidance, and interaction with a managed large language model.

Workflow Components

  1. Protection layer: The workflow begins with a protection component that helps prepare the incoming request for safe processing.
  2. PII identification: The Identify PII component detects personally identifiable information that may appear in the user's message.
  3. PII removal: The Remove PII component cleanses the text before it continues through the workflow, reducing the risk of exposing sensitive information.
  4. Session history: Previous conversation information is incorporated so that the agent can maintain context across multiple turns.
  5. Agent context: The Add Context component defines SafeStep's role as a cellphone and digital-help assistant for older adults and non-technical users.
  6. Task classification: A Python Sandbox component analyzes the user's request and determines the type of assistance being requested.
  7. Knowledge retrieval: Virtual Knowledge Base input and output components retrieve information relevant to the user's question.
  8. Variable management: Workflow variables preserve information such as the task result, user message, and safety classification.
  9. Safety guidance: A text component adds appropriate caution when a request involves payments, passwords, suspicious links, scams, or other higher-risk situations.
  10. Response generation: The processed request, contextual instructions, retrieved information, and safety guidance are sent to a managed large language model to produce the final response.

This modular design allowed SafeStep to combine conversational context, privacy protection, task classification, knowledge retrieval, safety rules, and response generation in one workflow. It also made the system easier to inspect and refine because each stage had a defined purpose.

Prompt Engineering

Prompt writing was central to the project because the prompts established how SafeStep should communicate and respond. The agent needed to remain helpful without overwhelming the user, cautious without becoming unresponsive, and supportive without implying that it could guarantee digital safety.

The prompt strategy emphasized several behavioral requirements:

  • Use simple and direct language.
  • Present instructions in a logical sequence.
  • Define technical terms when they cannot be avoided.
  • Ask clarifying questions when the user's request is ambiguous.
  • Warn users before potentially sensitive actions involving passwords, payments, downloads, or personal information.
  • Avoid claiming certainty when evaluating websites, messages, or security risks.
  • Encourage users to stop and seek trusted assistance when a situation appears unsafe.
  • Maintain a friendly, calm, patient, and nonjudgmental tone.

Illustrative Prompt Excerpt

The following shortened example illustrates the type of behavioral instructions used to define SafeStep's role and communication style: 

You are SafeStep, a digital-help assistant for older adults
and non-technical users.

Provide clear, patient, step-by-step guidance.

Use plain language and explain unfamiliar terms.

When a request involves money, passwords, downloads,
suspicious links, or personal information:

1. Explain the potential risk.
2. Avoid making guarantees about safety.
3. Recommend verification steps.
4. Encourage the user to stop if something appears suspicious.
5. Suggest contacting a trusted person or official support service
   when appropriate.

This excerpt is illustrative and summarizes the design principles used in the project rather than reproducing the complete platform configuration.

Task and Risk Classification

The workflow used a Python Sandbox component to distinguish ordinary device assistance from requests that required additional safety guidance. The classification process could identify categories such as mobile payments, suspicious links, scams, phishing, and general device help.

A simplified representation of the logic is shown below: 

task_category = "general_device_help"
risk_level = "low"
needs_safety_guidance = False

message_lower = user_message.lower()

if any(term in message_lower for term in [
    "pay", "payment", "bank", "transfer", "card"
]):
    task_category = "mobile_payments"
    risk_level = "medium"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

if any(term in message_lower for term in [
    "link", "website", "url", "click"
]):
    task_category = "suspicious_links"
    risk_level = "high"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

if any(term in message_lower for term in [
    "scam", "phishing", "password", "fraud"
]):
    task_category = "scam_or_phishing"
    risk_level = "high"
    needs_safety_guidance = True

This logic represents the type of classification used to route requests and determine whether the final response should include stronger warnings or verification guidance.

Testing and Iterative Refinement

I evaluated SafeStep through conversational testing rather than treating the initial workflow and prompt configuration as final. I entered representative questions and reviewed the resulting answers for clarity, usefulness, tone, contextual relevance, and appropriate safety language.

Testing scenarios included questions such as:

  • How to change a setting on a smartphone.
  • How to navigate an unfamiliar application or website.
  • Whether a message requesting personal information appeared suspicious.
  • What to verify before making an online payment.
  • How to respond to a possible phishing email.
  • How to interpret an unfamiliar warning or notification.

When testing revealed that an answer was too technical, too lengthy, too confident, or insufficiently tailored to the user, I revised the prompts and repeated the interaction. This iterative process helped me understand prompt engineering as a form of behavioral system design rather than a one-time writing task.

Knowledge and Information Sources

SafeStep was designed to work with information such as trusted cybersecurity guidance, application support documentation, public help pages, frequently asked questions, digital literacy materials, website metadata, screenshots, and structured safety checklists.

Although I did not create the knowledge base, I considered how the agent should use available information and communicate it responsibly. For an assistant dealing with digital safety, source quality and update frequency are particularly important because applications change and scam techniques continue to evolve.

The project concept therefore included periodic updates to application instructions and safety guidance, along with privacy-conscious access controls and minimal use of personal data.

Challenges and Design Decisions

Balancing Simplicity and Accuracy

The agent needed to make technical information understandable without removing details necessary for the user to complete a task correctly. My approach was to preserve essential information while reorganizing it into shorter steps and defining unfamiliar terms.

Avoiding False Confidence

A digital safety assistant must not imply that it can guarantee whether a website, message, or transaction is safe. SafeStep was therefore designed to identify warning signs, explain reasons for concern, and recommend verification steps rather than issue absolute judgments.

Handling Ambiguous Requests

Users may describe a device problem without naming the device, application, operating system, or exact screen they are viewing. The agent therefore needed to ask focused clarifying questions before providing detailed instructions.

Supporting Users Without Overwhelming Them

Long responses can be especially difficult for users who are already uncertain about a task. I addressed this by emphasizing short, ordered instructions and allowing the interaction to continue one stage at a time.

Designing for a Vulnerable Use Case

Some users may share screenshots or descriptions containing private information. The workflow's PII identification and removal stages were therefore especially relevant to the project's privacy goals.

Responsible AI and Risk Considerations

SafeStep was designed as a support tool rather than a substitute for human judgment or professional cybersecurity services. It may help users recognize suspicious patterns, but it cannot verify every website, anticipate every scam, or correctly interpret every unfamiliar interface.

Important risks considered during the project included:

  • Creating false confidence about whether a website or message is safe.
  • Exposing private or financial information through user-submitted content.
  • Misinterpreting screenshots, interfaces, or incomplete questions.
  • Encouraging overreliance on automated guidance.
  • Creating an interface that is itself inaccessible to the intended users.

These limitations influenced the agent's tone, workflow, and prompt instructions. The intended behavior was to explain, warn, and guide while clearly communicating uncertainty and encouraging users to seek trusted human assistance when appropriate.

Business and Social Value

SafeStep's primary value is its potential to support digital inclusion. Users who can complete online tasks more confidently may gain greater independence in communication, healthcare access, account management, payments, and other essential services.

Caregivers and family members may also benefit from having an additional source of accessible guidance available to the people they support. An organization deploying a similar assistant could potentially reduce repeated support requests while helping users complete tasks more successfully.

Possible measures of success include:

  • Higher rates of successful task completion.
  • Fewer user errors during common digital processes.
  • Improved user confidence when operating devices.
  • Greater recognition of suspicious websites or messages.
  • Reduced dependence on others for routine digital tasks.
  • Improved access to online services.
